\section{Introduction}


\textsc{MLRegTest} \citep{mlregtest} is a benchmark for learning regular languages from positive and negative data.
Using this benchmark, this paper compares the learning behavior of neural and state-merging learning algorithms on the regular languages, in particular exploring the role of string length in the algorithms' performance.
  
The performance of the state-merging algorithms RPNI, EDSM, and ALERGIA was examined by \citet{adil23} on a preliminary version of \textsc{MLRegTest} and found to be generally weaker than that of the neural learning models investigated by \citet{mlregtest}.
However, since \textsc{MLRegtTest} excludes strings less than length 19 in order to achieve a balance of positive and negative strings in its datasets, \citet{adil23} also report follow-up experiments which included shorter training strings and show that they dramatically improve the performance of the state-merging algorithms.
This indicates that string length may be an important factor in learning behavior for these algorithms, and opens the question of whether neural learning approaches are similarly impacted.
  
  In this context, this paper makes the following
  contributions. 
  First, we examine the performance of state merging algorithms on the final version of \textsc{MLRegTest}, not the preliminary version.
  Second, instead of exclusively running RPNI and EDSM out of the box, we conduct a limited gridsearch over the parameters provided
  by the flexfringe software \citep{flexfringe-short, flexfringe} to
  find suitable hyperparameters for merging states.
  Third, we report the results of this resulting state-merging algorithm on five experimental setups: short strings alone (\onlyShort), the small dataset of intermediate-length strings (\onlyInt \sm), the small dataset plus the short strings (\plusShort \sm), the medium dataset of intermediate-length strings (\onlyInt \med), and the medium dataset plus the short strings (\plusShort \med).
  Finally, we subject
  the neural baselines from \citet{mlregtest} to these same
  experiments for comparison.

In terms of overall accuracy, we find that the gated recurrent unit generally outperforms all other algorithms tested (both neural and state-merging) across experiments.
Interestingly, however, the state-merging algorithm outperforms the neural methods on regular languages that count modulo some positive integer. 
Additionally, the state-merging algorithm benefits much more from the addition of short strings than the neural models do--both in terms of performance and in terms of resilience to adversarial testing data.
The neural approaches, meanwhile, are far more sensitive to longer strings and see only moderate improvement with the addition of short ones. 

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:bg} introduces the learning algorithms that we examine, both the state-merging (\ref{sec:ff}) and neural (\ref{sec:neural}) systems.
Section~\ref{sec:eval} summarizes the setup of the \textsc{MLRegTest} datasets, explains our addition of short strings to these datasets, and outlines our full experimental paradigm.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports our findings, examining the effects of string length (\ref{sec:res-short}), type of test set (\ref{sec:res-test}), specific language class (\ref{sec:res-class}), and the logical levels and ordering relations those classes are built on (\ref{sec:res-relation}).
Finally, Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} offers ideas for future directions and concludes.
  
\section{Learning Systems} \label{sec:bg}


\subsection{State-Merging Systems}\label{sec:ff}

Following \citet{adil23}, we use the state-merging software
\textsc{FlexFringe} \citep{flexfringe},\footnote{We use the same
  version of \textsc{FlexFringe} used by \citeauthor{adil23}: commit
  \texttt{4d1449c} of the software available
  \href{https://github.com/tudelft-cda-lab/FlexFringe/commit/4d1449c4d0cfb3fc5f9a954d5c3a24a11a593a5c\#diff-67290ef9e84d355c83cbd9a0bda3be39e2e14836990458cfcc070506a7e448c2}{here}.
} which implements a range of state-merging strategies using the
red/blue framework (see \citet{Higuera2010}). For instance, the
algorithms EDSM \citep{edsm} and RPNI \citep{rpni} are particular
strategies which \textsc{FlexFringe} can instantiate.  Generally
speaking, these algorithms construct a prefix tree from their input
and search for pairs of states to merge.

In general, \textsc{FlexFringe} scores pairs of states for their
``mergability'' and merges states with sufficient scores in some
order, as determined by various parameter
settings. \textsc{FlexFringe} also can also learn stochastic regular
languages with state-merging algorithms such as ALERGIA
\citep{CarrascoOncina1994, carr99}. However, we exclude ALERGIA from
consideration because of its comparatively low performance noted by
\citeauthor{adil23}.

\textsc{FlexFringe} provides several parameters which determine its
state-merging strategy.  To find the best model, we conducted a
limited gridsearch over the following subset: \texttt{PerformFirst}
(EDSM vs. RPNI), \texttt{Extend} (color blue states red that cannot be
merged with any red), \texttt{ShallowFirst} (consider coloring blue
states closest to the root first), and \texttt{SearchStrategy}
(searchdeep, searchlocal, searchglobal, searchpartial, or none).  Grid
searching was done on the small partition of the \textsc{PlusShort}
data using overall accuracy on the \textsc{Small} development set
(these data sets are described in \S\ref{sec:eval}). The best
performing model, used in the remainder of the paper, was
\texttt{PerformFirst = 0} (i.e., RPNI), \texttt{Extend = 1},
\texttt{ShallowFirst = 0}, and \texttt{SearchStrategy = SearchDeep}.

The SearchDeep strategy deserves some discussion. This strategy
considers the tree of all possible sequences of merges and the sizes
of the resulting DFA. It ultimately returns the DFA with the fewest
number of states. Given that the tree representing all possible
sequences of merges is quite large (and grows larger with the size of
the initial prefix tree), it is time-consuming to run.

We held several other parameters at their default state because
modifying them led to errors in the \texttt{FlexFringe}
executable. These are: \texttt{ReverseTraces}, \texttt{DepthCheck},
\texttt{SymbolCheck}, \texttt{SinksOn}, \texttt{SinkCount}, and
\texttt{MergeScore}. However, we have since learned more about these
parameters (Sicco Verweer, p.c.), and we believe we can avoid these
errors in the future. We plan to run a more comprehensive grid search
this summer.

\subsection{Neural Systems} \label{sec:neural}
Following \citet{mlregtest}, we test four neural architectures, all
consisting of a trainable embedding layer followed by a unidirectional
recurrent module (either a \textbf{\textsc{Simple}} RNN, a
\textbf{GRU}, or an \textbf{LSTM}) or two consecutive
\textbf{\textsc{Transformer}} blocks, dense feed-forward layers,
dropout, layer normalization, and softmax activation.  All NNs use the
same hyperparameters as \citeauthor{mlregtest}.\footnote{The
  implementations of the neural networks are available at
  \href{https://github.com/heinz-jeffrey/subregular-learning/}{https://github.com/heinz-jeffrey/subregular-learning/}}

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:eval}

\textsc{MLRegTest} \citep{mlregtest} contains training, development,
and test data from 1,800 regular languages. The languages were
organized along three dimensions: alphabet size, logical complexity,
and model-theoretic representations of the ordering relation in
strings. The logical complexity and ordering relations gave rise to 16
distinct classes, shown in Table~\ref{tab:classes} (reproduced from
\citet{adil23}).

Five logics are considered: Monadic Second Order (MSO), First Order
(FO), Propositional (Prop), Conjunctions of Negative literals (CNL)
and Disjunctions of Positive Literals (DPL).  CNL and DPL are
complements of the other and both are properly contained within Prop,
which is properly contained in FO, which is contained within MSO.  The
three model-theoretic ordering relations are successor
($\triangleleft$), tier-successor ($\triangleleft_T$), and precedence
($<$), which correspond to the concepts of substring, tier-substring,
and subsequence, respectively.  A tier $T$ is a subset of the alphabet
which can lead to a change of state. Non-tier elements are neutral and
can be freely inserted or deleted \citep{Lambert2023}. \textsc{MLRegTest}
considers an additional subclass of purely periodic languages they
call Zp. For example, the language whose words contain an even number
of $a$ symbols belongs to Zp. 

The names of these classes are given in Table~\ref{tab:classes},
alongside their logical characterization. These classes and their
relationships are discussed by many, including
\citet{McNaughtonPapert1971, Thomas1982, Thomas1997, RogersPullum2011,
  Rogers-HeinzEtAl-2013-CSC, rogers-lambert-2019-classes}. See
\citet{Lambert+2021-TESRL} for an approach to learning these classes
individually. Readers are referred to \citet{mlregtest} for additional
details regarding these language classes.

\citet{mlregtest} use the Language Toolkit \citep{Lambert2024} to
verify that every language in some class A in \textsc{MLRegTest} belongs to A
and does not belong to any other class B, where B is a subclass of A
or is incomparable with A.

The datasets for each language come in three sizes: \sm{}
(1,000 strings), \med{} (10,000 strings), and \lrg{}
(100,000 strings). For each language, the datasets are nested;
\textsc{Small} is contained in \textsc{Medium}, which is contained in
\textsc{Large}. Each dataset includes equal numbers of positive and
negative strings.

The \textsc{MLRegTest} training sets (here referred to as \orig{}) contain strings of lengths 20-29, sampled with
replacement. The development sets also contain strings of lengths
20-29, sampled without replacement in a manner to keep the development
sets disjoint from the training set.
 
% \citeauthor{adil23} found that augmenting the training set with up to 1,000 positive and negative strings of length 1-19 significantly improved \textsc{FlexFringe} performance despite sacrificing the balance of positive and negative examples.
\citeauthor{adil23} found that augmenting the training set with strings of length 1-19 significantly improved \textsc{FlexFringe} performance.
\citeauthor{adil23} built a short training set for each language as
follows. 
For each string length (1-19), 25 positive and 25 negative strings were
sampled with replacement.
If no positive negative strings of a given length existed, none were drawn for that length.
This method resulted in a supplemental \short{} training set for each language, containing up to 1,000 shorter strings.
It is worth noting that a key difference between these \short{} sets and the \orig{} sets originally included in \textsc{MLRegTest} is that for many Regular languages, there is no guarantee that 25 positive (negative) strings can be generated for a given length.
For example, many Regular languages contain \emph{no} strings of length 1 or 2.
This has three important implications for the nature of the \sm{} testing sets.
Firstly, most \sm{} test sets contain significantly less than 1,000 examples, and the number of examples is not standardized across languages.
Second, due to the small numbers of positive (negative) strings for certain lengths and the procedure of sampling with replacement, specific strings are more likely to be repeated several times in the \sm{} test sets.
Finally, the \sm{} test sets are not balanced between numbers of positive and negative strings.
Some contain mostly positive examples, and some mostly negative.
All these properties are generally undesirable in training data sets for machine learning, and the original \textsc{MLRegTest} excluded shorter string lengths for exactly this reason.
This work, however, is intended to explicitly examine the impact of shorter strings on the learning of regular languages, and we therefore consider the \sm{} training data to be nevertheless valuable.

\textsc{MLRegTest} contains four test sets per language, based on the
string length and the method of generation. 
\orig{} test sets have strings of length 20-29, matching the training data, while \lng{} sets have strings of length
31-50. 
\textsc{Random} test sets were created by sampling without replacement in a manner to keep them disjoint from the corresponding training and development sets. 
\textsc{Adversarial} sets sampled pairs of positive and negative examples with an edit distance of 1. 
Like the \textsc{Random} test sets, these were also sampled in a manner to keep them disjoint from the training and
development sets. 
(Of course for the \lng{} test sets nothing
needed to be done to ensure they were disjoint from the training and
development sets, since these only included strings of shorter lengths.)
Henceforth, we refer to these test sets as \origRand, \longRand, \origAdv, \longAdv for \textsc{\origRandFull, \longRandFull, \origAdvFull}, and \textsc{\longAdvFull}, respectively.

Following \citet{mlregtest} and \citet{adil23}, we evaluate trained models only on the \lrg{} test sets (recall that the sets are nested, so the \lrg{} test sets contain the \med{} and \sm{} ones).
Note that these test sets are limited to strings of \orig{} or \lng{} length, and \short{} strings are not included in testing.
This means that the undesirable properties of the \short{} sets described above are limited to our training regime, and do not impact our testing setup (in particular, our test sets maintain their balance between positive and negative examples).
The purpose of including \short{} strings in training is to investigate how much these strings help models to generalize, and the \orig{} and \lng{} testing data are adequate to evaluate this.

In their preliminary experiments, \citeauthor{adil23} used an early, unfinished version of \textsc{MLRegTest} and set the parameters of the \textsc{FlexFringe} model to fixed default settings.
In this work, we replicate their analyses on the final, published version
\footnote{MLRegTest is publicly available with Dryad \url{https://doi.org/10.5061/dryad.dncjsxm4h} under the license \emph{CC0 1.0 Universal (CC0 1.0) Public Domain Dedication} (\url{https://creativecommons.org/publicdomain/zero/1.0/}).}, 
as well as conducting a gridsearch to identify the best-performing \textsc{FlexFringe} parameter settings and extending their experiments to provide a comparison against neural network models on the \short{} datasets.

We conducted three kinds of experiments. 
In the \onlyShort experiments, only the short training set was used for training. 
In the \plusShort experiments, the training set was made up of the original \textsc{MLRegTest} training sets augmented with the short
training set. 
In the \onlyInt experiments, the original \textsc{MLRegTest} training sets were used (without the short training set).

We excluded languages with alphabet sizes of 64, thus considering
1,140, in order to compare our results with \citet{adil23}. 
This submission also excludes the Large training sets because the
SearchDeep strategy caused \textsc{FlexFringe} to run too long during
training (see \ref{sec:conclusion} for notes on future work in this direction).

Thus for each language, for each learning system, we considered five
training regimes: \onlyShort, \textsc{Small} \plusShort, \textsc{Small} \onlyInt, \textsc{Medium} \plusShort, \textsc{Medium} \onlyInt.
Each trained model was tested on
the aforementioned four test sets.
  
% Terminology recap
Note that we have now introduced terminology to categorize data sets according to two properties: the \emph{total number} of strings they contain, and the \emph{lengths} of these strings.
The terms \sm{}, \med{}{}, and \lrg{} refer to the number of examples in a dataset (1,000, 10,000, and 100,000 examples respectively).
Meanwhile, the terms \short{}, \orig{}, and \lng{} refer to the lengths of the strings in a set (1-19, 20-29, and 30-39 characters respectively).

The \plusShort and \onlyInt experiments parallel the experiments in \citet{adil23} on a preliminary version of \textsc{MLRegTest}, with the additions of using \textsc{FlexFringe} parameter settings established by a gridsearch (see \ref{sec:ff}) and including neural models in the experimental setup. 
The \onlyShort experiments for both \textsc{FlexFringe} and the neural models are novel to this paper and help clarify the role short training strings play for learning regular languages.

\section{Results} \label{sec:results}

We use the same non-parametric methods as \citet{mlregtest} and \citet{adil23} in our analysis, as advocated by \citet{Demsar2006} and \citet{Stapor2017}. 
% We report aggregate accuracy for the models as the primary measurement of performance since the test sets are balanced, and very strong correlations were found across performance measures. 
We report aggregate accuracy as the primary measurement of performance for all models.
Given the balanced nature of the tests sets, very strong correlations were found across performance measures.
Experimental observations are aggregated over the variables
\Tier, \kay, \jay, \eye and \Alpha. These are held as blocking
variables, and factors \Class, \TrainSize, \TestType, and \AlgoType
are treatment variables which average accuracy is computed over. The
Friedman rank sum test is used to determine if the factor being held
for treatment contributes significantly to predicting model accuracy
by holding the null hypothesis that all variations of the value
contribute equally. Additional analysis using the
Nemenyi-Wilcoxon-Wilcox all-pairs test identified which pairs differ
significantly.

The GRU consistently outperforms the other models across all

\textsc{Medium Plus Short} condition.

Another important observation is that without the Short training data
(e.g. comparing \textsc{Small Plus Short} with \textsc{Only Small}),
\textsc{FlexFringe}'s performance drops at least 20 points. On the
other hand, the NNs, across the board, are much less sensitive to the
inclusion of short training data. The largest drop is about 12 points
for the LSTM when comparing \textsc{Small Plus Short} with

A third important observation is that there is a larger jump in
performance for the NNs from the \textsc{Small} to \textsc{Medium}
training conditions than there is for \textsc{FlexFringe}. From
\textsc{Small Plus Short} to \textsc{Medium Plus Short},
\textsc{FlexFringe}'s performance increases by about only 2 points,
whereas the NNs' performances increase about 5 points, except for the
LSTM which shows a nearly 15 point increase.

Taken together, these results suggest that \textsc{FlexFringe}
benefits far more from the inclusion of short strings, while the
neural models rely more on long strings to achieve their
performance. This seems to be particularly true for the GRU and LSTM
which see the large perfomance increases in the \textsc{Small} and
\textsc{Medium} conditions.

additional training data, the neural models, and especially the GRU,
become more resilient to adversarial tests.

\subsection{FlexFringe Performance by Logical Level and Order Relation} \label{sec:res-relation}

We also checked to see whether the logical level or the ordering
relation was a significant factor. The languages are grouped by the
logical level as shown in Table~\ref{tab:classes}, with the addition
that Zp is grouped together with Reg. The Friedman rank sum test is
not significant (Friedman chi-squared = 6.95, df = 4, p-value = 0.1386).

To examine the ordering relation, we grouped the languages into four
categories as shown in Table~\ref{tab:order-relation}.

signficance threshold, we also report Nemenyi-Wilcoxon-Wilcox

unexpected given that the state-merging algorithms it is based

deterministic finite-state automata.

\section{Conclusions}  \label{sec:conclusion}
The strongest model overall was the GRU, which performed best across
partitions.  Strikingly, however, the performance of the state merging
algorithm benefited far more from the addition of short strings than
that of any of the neural models: it performed most competitively on
\textsc{OnlyShort}.  Meanwhile, increasing the size of the training
set was less helpful for the state merging algorithm than for the
neural models.
