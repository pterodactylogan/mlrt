\begin{abstract}%
	
	We test the performance of state merging algorithms against four neural network architectures on \textsc{MLRegTest}, a benchmark for learning regular languages from positive and negative data \citep{mlregtest}.
	Prior work by \citet{adil23} on a preliminary version of \textsc{MLRegTest} found that neural learning models generally outperformed state merging algorithms on this benchmark.
	However, \citet{adil23} also note that \textsc{MLRegTest} excludes strings shorter than length 20 and report a follow-up experiment demonstrating that the addition of shorter strings dramatically improves the performance of state merging algorithms.
        They did not examine the performance of neural models with shorter strings.
        
	We expand on this work by conducting several experiments using the published version of \textsc{MLRegTest}.
	Firstly, we conduct a gridsearch over the hyperparameters provided by the \textsc{FlexFringe} software \citep{flexfringe-short, flexfringe} to identify the best-performing state merging system.
	We then report the results of this state merging algorithm on experimental setups which vary by training set size, inclusion of shorter training strings, and inclusion of intermediate-length training strings.
	We also subject the neural baselines from \citet{mlregtest} to these same experiments, and compare the performance between these and the state merging algorithm.
	
	We conclude that the gated recurrent unit has the strongest performance across the benchmarks, with some interesting caveats.
	Namely, the state merging algorithm outperforms all neural architectures on regular languages which count modulo some positive integer.
	Additionally, the state merging algorithm sees much greater improvement and more resiliency to adversarial test sets from the inclusion of shorter strings, while the neural models do much better with longer strings and larger data samples.
	These results suggest that state merging approaches may be more attuned to learning from shorter, smaller input data, while neural learners need the opposite.

\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
  state-merging, regular languages, finite-state automata, neural networks
\end{keywords}


\section{Introduction}


\textsc{MLRegTest} \citep{mlregtest} is a benchmark for learning regular languages from positive and negative data.
Using this benchmark, this paper compares the learning behavior of neural and state-merging learning algorithms on the regular languages, in particular exploring the role of string length in the algorithms' performance.
  
The performance of the state-merging algorithms RPNI, EDSM, and ALERGIA was examined by \citet{adil23} on a preliminary version of \textsc{MLRegTest} and found to be generally weaker than that of the neural learning models investigated by \citet{mlregtest}.
However, since \textsc{MLRegTest} excludes strings of length 19 or shorter in order to achieve a balance of positive and negative strings in its datasets, \citet{adil23} also report follow-up experiments which included shorter training strings and show that they dramatically improve the performance of the state-merging algorithms.
This indicates that string length may be an important factor in learning behavior for these algorithms, and opens the question of whether neural learning approaches are similarly impacted.
  
  In this context, this paper makes the following
  contributions. 
  First, we examine the performance of state merging algorithms on the final version of \textsc{MLRegTest}, not the preliminary version.
  Second, instead of exclusively running RPNI and EDSM out of the box, we conduct a limited gridsearch over the parameters provided
  by the flexfringe software \citep{flexfringe-short, flexfringe} to
  find suitable hyperparameters for merging states.
  Third, we report the results of this resulting state-merging algorithm on five experimental setups: short strings alone (\onlyShort), the small-sized dataset of intermediate-length strings (\sm \onlyInt), the small-sized dataset plus the short strings (\sm \plusShort), the medium-sized dataset of intermediate-length strings (\med \onlyInt), and the medium-sized dataset plus the short strings (\med \plusShort).
  Finally, we subject
  the neural baselines from \citet{mlregtest} to these same
  experiments for comparison.

In terms of overall accuracy, we find that the gated recurrent unit generally outperforms all other algorithms tested (both neural and state-merging) across experiments.
Interestingly, however, the state-merging algorithm outperforms the neural methods on regular languages that count modulo some positive integer. 
Additionally, the state-merging algorithm benefits much more from the addition of short strings than the neural models do--both in terms of performance and in terms of resilience to adversarial testing data.
The neural approaches, meanwhile, are far more sensitive to longer strings and see only moderate improvement with the addition of short ones. 

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:bg} introduces the learning algorithms that we examine, both the state-merging (\S\ref{sec:ff}) and neural (\S\ref{sec:neural}) systems.
Section~\ref{sec:eval} summarizes the setup of the \textsc{MLRegTest} datasets, explains our addition of short strings to these datasets, and outlines our full experimental paradigm.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports our findings, examining the effects of string length (\S\ref{sec:res-short}), type of test set (\S\ref{sec:res-test}), specific language class (\S\ref{sec:res-class}), and the logical levels and ordering relations those classes are built on (\S\ref{sec:res-relation}).
Finally, Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} offers ideas for future directions and concludes.
  
\section{Learning Systems} \label{sec:bg}


\subsection{State-Merging Systems}\label{sec:ff}

Following \citet{adil23}, we use the state-merging software
\textsc{FlexFringe} \citep{flexfringe},\footnote{We use the same
  version of \textsc{FlexFringe} used by \citeauthor{adil23}: commit
  \texttt{4d1449c} of the software available
  \href{https://github.com/tudelft-cda-lab/FlexFringe/commit/4d1449c4d0cfb3fc5f9a954d5c3a24a11a593a5c\#diff-67290ef9e84d355c83cbd9a0bda3be39e2e14836990458cfcc070506a7e448c2}{here}.
} which implements a range of state-merging strategies using the
red/blue framework (see \citet{Higuera2010}). 
Generally speaking, these algorithms take as input a list of strings each labeled as to whether or not they belong in the target language.
They first construct a prefix tree from this input, with accepting and non-accepting states notated accordingly.
Then, states in the prefix tree are merged together until finally, a finite state acceptor is returned which represents the learned grammar.
In general, pairs of states are scored for their ``mergability'', and states with sufficient scores are merged in a particular order as determined by the specific hyperparameter settings being used.
These different hyperparameter settings correspond to different individual state-merging algorithms.
For example, the EDSM \citep{edsm} and RPNI \citep{rpni} algorithms are particular strategies among the many which \textsc{FlexFringe} can instantiate.
\textsc{FlexFringe} also can also learn stochastic regular
languages with state-merging algorithms such as ALERGIA
\citep{CarrascoOncina1994, carr99}. 
However, we exclude ALERGIA from
consideration because of its comparatively low performance noted by
\citeauthor{adil23}.

\textsc{FlexFringe} provides several parameters which determine its
state-merging strategy.  To find the best model, we conducted a
limited gridsearch over the following subset: \texttt{PerformFirst}
(EDSM vs. RPNI), \texttt{Extend} (color blue states red that cannot be
merged with any red), \texttt{ShallowFirst} (consider coloring blue
states closest to the root first), and \texttt{SearchStrategy}
(searchdeep, searchlocal, searchglobal, searchpartial, or none).  
Grid searching was done on the small partition of the \textsc{PlusShort}
data using overall accuracy on the \lrg development set
(these data sets are described in \S\ref{sec:eval}). 
The best
performing model, used in the remainder of the paper, was
\texttt{PerformFirst = 0} (i.e., RPNI), \texttt{Extend = 1},
\texttt{ShallowFirst = 0}, and \texttt{SearchStrategy = SearchDeep}.
These settings for \texttt{ShallowFirst} and \texttt{SearchStrategy} represent a departure from the default settings used by \citet{adil23}, which were \texttt{ShallowFirst = 1}, and \texttt{SearchStrategy = none}.

The SearchDeep strategy deserves some discussion. 
This strategy considers the tree of all possible sequences of merges and the sizes of the resulting finite state acceptors. 
It ultimately returns the acceptor with the fewest number of states. 
Given that the tree representing all possible sequences of merges is quite large (and grows larger with the size of the initial prefix tree), it is time-consuming to run.
Notably, without this parameter setting, the \textsc{FlexFringe} models typically run in a matter of seconds (compared to minutes or hours for the neural models).

We held several other parameters at their default state because
modifying them led to errors in the \texttt{FlexFringe}
executable. These are: \texttt{ReverseTraces}, \texttt{DepthCheck},
\texttt{SymbolCheck}, \texttt{SinksOn}, \texttt{SinkCount}, and
\texttt{MergeScore}. 
However, we have since learned more about these
parameters (Sicco Verweer, p.c.), and we believe these errors can be avoided in future work (see Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} for a discussion of future research directions).

\subsection{Neural Systems} \label{sec:neural}
Following \citet{mlregtest}, we test four neural architectures, all
consisting of a trainable embedding layer followed by a unidirectional
recurrent module (either a \textbf{\textsc{Simple}} RNN, a
\textbf{GRU}, or an \textbf{LSTM}) or two consecutive
\textbf{\textsc{Transformer}} blocks, dense feed-forward layers,
dropout, layer normalization, and softmax activation. 
We trained these neural models on the same datasets as the \textsc{FlexFringe} models (i.e., lists of strings labeled according to membership in the target language), and provided the development sets for validation.
All neural networks use the
same hyperparameters as \citeauthor{mlregtest}\footnote{The
  implementations of the neural networks are available at
  \href{https://github.com/heinz-jeffrey/subregular-learning/}{https://github.com/heinz-jeffrey/subregular-learning/}}, which were identified using a grid search to find the best-performing settings.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:eval}

\textsc{MLRegTest} \citep{mlregtest} contains training, development,
and test data from 1,800 regular languages. The languages were
organized along three dimensions: alphabet size, logical complexity,
and model-theoretic representations of the ordering relation in
strings. The logical complexity and ordering relations gave rise to 16
distinct classes, shown in Table~\ref{tab:classes} (reproduced from
\citet{adil23}).

Five logics are considered: Monadic Second Order (MSO), First Order
(FO), Propositional (Prop), Conjunctions of Negative literals (CNL)
and Disjunctions of Positive Literals (DPL).  CNL and DPL are
complements of the other and both are properly contained within Prop,
which is properly contained in FO, which is contained within MSO.  The
three model-theoretic ordering relations are successor
($\triangleleft$), tier-successor ($\triangleleft_T$), and precedence
($<$), which correspond to the concepts of substring, tier-substring,
and subsequence, respectively.  A tier $T$ is a subset of the alphabet
which can lead to a change of state. Non-tier elements are neutral and
can be freely inserted or deleted \citep{Lambert2023}. \textsc{MLRegTest}
considers an additional subclass of purely periodic languages they
call Zp. For example, the language whose words contain an even number
of $a$ symbols belongs to Zp. 

The names of these classes are given in Table~\ref{tab:classes},
alongside their logical characterization. These classes and their
relationships are discussed by many, including
\citet{McNaughtonPapert1971, Thomas1982, Thomas1997, RogersPullum2011,
  Rogers-HeinzEtAl-2013-CSC, rogers-lambert-2019-classes}. See
\citet{Lambert+2021-TESRL} for an approach to learning these classes
individually. Readers are referred to \citet{mlregtest} for additional
details regarding these language classes.

\citet{mlregtest} use the Language Toolkit \citep{Lambert2024} to
verify that every language in some class A in \textsc{MLRegTest} belongs to A
and does not belong to any other class B, where B is a subclass of A
or is incomparable with A.

The datasets for each language come in three sizes: \sm
(1,000 strings), \med (10,000 strings), and \lrg
(100,000 strings). For each language, the datasets are nested;
\textsc{Small} is contained in \textsc{Medium}, which is contained in
\textsc{Large}. Each dataset includes equal numbers of positive and
negative strings.

The \textsc{MLRegTest} training sets contain strings of lengths 20-29, sampled with
replacement (these sets are referred to here as \orig, to distinguish them from sets of shorter or longer strings). 
The development sets also contain strings of lengths
20-29, sampled without replacement in a manner to keep the development
sets disjoint from the training set.
 
% \citeauthor{adil23} found that augmenting the training set with up to 1,000 positive and negative strings of length 1-19 significantly improved \textsc{FlexFringe} performance despite sacrificing the balance of positive and negative examples.
\citeauthor{adil23} found that augmenting the training set with strings of length 1-19 significantly improved \textsc{FlexFringe} performance.
\citeauthor{adil23} built a training set of short strings for each language as
follows. 
For each string length (1-19), 25 positive and 25 negative strings were
sampled with replacement.
If no positive negative strings of a given length existed, none were drawn for that length.
This method resulted in a supplemental \short training set for each language, containing up to 1,000 shorter strings.
It is worth noting that a key difference between these \short sets and the \orig sets originally included in \textsc{MLRegTest} is that for many regular languages, there is no guarantee that 25 positive (negative) strings can be generated for a given length.
For example, many regular languages contain \emph{no} strings of length 1 or 2.

This has three important implications for the nature of the \sm{} testing sets.
Firstly, most \sm{} test sets contain significantly less than 1,000 examples, and the number of examples is not standardized across languages.
Second, due to the small numbers of positive (negative) strings for certain lengths and the procedure of sampling with replacement, specific strings are more likely to be repeated several times in the \sm{} test sets.
Finally, the \sm{} test sets are not balanced between numbers of positive and negative strings.
Some contain mostly positive examples, and some mostly negative.
The original \textsc{MLRegTest} excluded shorter string lengths in order to run controlled experiments with balanced training sets.
This work, however, is intended to explicitly examine the impact of shorter strings on the learning of regular languages, and we therefore consider the \sm{} training data to be valuable.

\textsc{MLRegTest} contains four test sets per language, based on the
string length and the method of generation. 
\orig{} test sets have strings of length 20-29, matching the training data, while \lng{} sets have strings of length
31-50. 
\textsc{Random} test sets were created by sampling without replacement in a manner to keep them disjoint from the corresponding training and development sets. 
\textsc{Adversarial} sets sampled pairs of positive and negative examples with an edit distance of 1. 
Like the \textsc{Random} test sets, these were also sampled in a manner to keep them disjoint from the training and
development sets. 
(Of course for the \lng{} test sets nothing
needed to be done to ensure they were disjoint from the training and
development sets, since these only included strings of shorter lengths.)
Henceforth, we refer to these test sets as \origRand, \longRand, \origAdv, \longAdv for \textsc{\origRandFull, \longRandFull, \origAdvFull}, and \textsc{\longAdvFull}, respectively.\footnote{In \citet{mlregtest}, these were called Short Random (SR), Long Random (LR), Short Adversarial (SA), and Long Adversarial (LA), respectively.}

Following \citet{mlregtest} and \citet{adil23}, we evaluate trained models only on the \lrg test sets (recall that the sets are nested, so the \lrg test sets contain the \med and \sm ones).
Note that these test sets are limited to strings of \orig or \lng length, with \short strings absent from testing.
This means that the undesirable properties of the \short sets described above are limited to our training regime, and do not impact our testing setup (in particular, our test sets maintain their balance between positive and negative examples).
The purpose of including \short strings in training is to investigate how much these strings help models to generalize, and the \orig{} and \lng{} testing data are adequate to evaluate this.

% In this work, we rebuilt the short training sets using that same recipe because \citeauthor{adil23} used a preliminary version of \textsc{MLRegTest}, and we used the published version.\footnote{MLRegTest is publicly available with Dryad \url{https://doi.org/10.5061/dryad.dncjsxm4h} under the license \emph{CC0 1.0 Universal (CC0 1.0) Public Domain Dedication} (\url{https://creativecommons.org/publicdomain/zero/1.0/}).}
In their preliminary experiments, \citeauthor{adil23} used an early, unfinished version of \textsc{MLRegTest} which included issues with string sampling (see footnote 5 on page 19 of \citet{mlregtest}) as well as some errors in the underlying automata for generating certain languages.
Additionally, \citeauthor{adil23} tested only three state merging variants (corresponding to the RPNI, EDSM, and ALERGIA algorithms) by setting the parameters of the \textsc{FlexFringe} model to fixed default settings.
In this work, we replicate their analyses on the final, published version of \textsc{MLRegTest}
\footnote{MLRegTest is publicly available with Dryad \url{https://doi.org/10.5061/dryad.dncjsxm4h} under the license \emph{CC0 1.0 Universal (CC0 1.0) Public Domain Dedication} (\url{https://creativecommons.org/publicdomain/zero/1.0/}).}, 
as well as conducting a gridsearch to identify the best-performing \textsc{FlexFringe} parameter settings.
We also extend their experiments to provide a comparison against neural network models on the \short datasets.

We conducted three kinds of experiments. 
In the \onlyShort experiments, only the short training set was used for training. 
In the \plusShort experiments, the training set was made up of the original \textsc{MLRegTest} training sets augmented with the short
training set. 
In the \onlyInt experiments, the original \textsc{MLRegTest} training sets were used (without the short training set).

We excluded languages with alphabet sizes of 64, thus considering
1,140, in order to compare our results with \citet{adil23}. 
This submission also excludes the Large training sets because the
\texttt{SearchDeep} strategy caused \textsc{FlexFringe} to run too long during
training (see \S\ref{sec:conclusion} for notes on future work in this direction).

Thus for each language, for each learning algorithm, we considered five
training regimes: \onlyShort, \sm \plusShort, \sm \onlyInt, \med \plusShort, and \med \onlyInt.
Each trained model was tested on
the aforementioned four test sets.
  
% Terminology recap
Note that we have now introduced terminology to categorize data sets according to two properties: the \emph{total number} of strings they contain, and the \emph{lengths} of these strings.
The terms \sm{}, \med{}{}, and \lrg{} refer to the number of examples in a dataset (1,000, 10,000, and 100,000 examples respectively).
Meanwhile, the terms \short{}, \orig{}, and \lng{} refer to the lengths of the strings in a set (1-19, 20-29, and 31-50 characters respectively).

The \plusShort and \onlyInt experiments parallel the experiments in \citet{adil23} on a preliminary version of \textsc{MLRegTest}, with the additions of using \textsc{FlexFringe} parameter settings established by a gridsearch (see \S\ref{sec:ff}) and including neural models in the experimental setup. 
The \onlyShort experiments for both \textsc{FlexFringe} and the neural models are novel to this paper and help clarify the role short training strings play for learning regular languages.

\section{Results} \label{sec:results}

We use the same non-parametric methods as \citet{mlregtest} and \citet{adil23} in our analysis, as advocated by \citet{Demsar2006} and \citet{Stapor2017}. 
% We report aggregate accuracy for the models as the primary measurement of performance since the test sets are balanced, and very strong correlations were found across performance measures. 
We report aggregate accuracy as the primary measurement of performance for all models.
Given the balanced nature of the tests sets, very strong correlations were found across performance measures.
Experimental observations are aggregated over the variables
\Tier, \kay, \jay, \eye and \Alpha. These are held as blocking
variables, and factors \Class, \TrainSize, \TestType, and \AlgoType
are treatment variables which average accuracy is computed over. The
Friedman rank sum test is used to determine if the factor being held
for treatment contributes significantly to predicting model accuracy
by holding the null hypothesis that all variations of the value
contribute equally. Additional analysis using the
Nemenyi-Wilcoxon-Wilcox all-pairs test identified which pairs differ
significantly.

every model (\(p < \num{2.2e-16}\)). The results in the
\plusShort and \onlyInt experiments are consistent
with \citet{adil23} and \citet{mlregtest}, respectively.

The differences between accuracy in the IR and IA test sets, and the differences between LR and LA test sets were examined separately. In general, the paired t-test should be used for normally distributed data when the numbers of data points are small. As the number of data points increases,
he normal population assumption becomes less important
because of the Central Limit Theorem \citep[pp. 119, 192-3]{Agresti2018}. Here, the histograms did not appear normally distribution, but there were over a thousand data points being analyzed, so we concluded the paired t-test was appropriate.

In sum, when only short strings are provided, \textsc{FlexFringe} is more
resilient to adversarial test sets than the neural models. 
With longer strings and additional training data, the neural models, and especially the GRU, become more resilient to adversarial tests.

\section{Conclusions}  \label{sec:conclusion}
The strongest model overall was the GRU, which performed best across partitions.
Strikingly, however, the state merging algorithm benefited far more from the addition of short strings than any of the neural models, seeing major jumps in performance between the \onlyInt and \plusShort conditions.
In fact, the state merging algorithm performed most competitively in the \onlyShort and \sm \plusShort conditions, where it outperformed all neural models except the GRU.
Additionally, the state merging algorithm showed particularly impressive performance on the Zp class of languages (characterized by modulo counting), where it outperformed all other models.
The neural models, meanwhile, were much more sensitive to the size of the training set, seeing much greater improvement between the \sm and \med training sizes than the state merging algorithm.

These results indicate an attunement of the state merging approach to certain kinds of input samples: namely, small data and short strings.
The neural methods, meanwhile, are more suited to large datasets and long strings.
This finding is interesting because, in some settings, overall accuracy is not the only important factor when evaluating what makes a good learning model.
In terms of modeling how humans learn natural language, for example, leveraging small data and short strings is a crucial property.
Modulo counting, on the other hand, is generally considered to be outside the domain of human language patterns, so the success of the state-merging algorithm on the Zp languages may actually suggest it is in some ways \emph{too} powerful to be a good model for human language learning.
State-merging algorithms which target aperiodic regular languages may therefore be of particular interest.
Overall, the results of these experiments allow us to paint a more nuanced picture of which properties are relevant for learning regular languages and how different algorithms respond to different learning setups.

Future research can continue to expand and refine these findings in several ways.
Firstly, as noted in Section~\ref{sec:ff}, the gridsearch we conducted was limited to a small handful of the hyperparameters provided by \textsc{FlexFringe}.
Newer versions of \textsc{FlexFringe} make it possible to vary more of these parameters and run a more complete gridsearch.
This will offer further insight about what state merging strategies work best for regular language learning, as well as potentially yielding a better-performing state merging algorithm.
Relatedly, while the results of our gridsearch suggest that the \texttt{SearchDeep} search strategy may offer the best performance, this strategy is too time-consuming to use at a large scale without in some way limiting its search.
As explained in Section~\ref{sec:ff}, the \texttt{SearchDeep} strategy is not just performing merges in a single prefix tree, but actually constructing a tree of all possible merges to find the smallest resulting finite state acceptor.
For the \lrg data, this quickly becomes impractical.
To maintain the advantages of this strategy and still allow for scalability, this search of possible merges can be terminated after a specific time interval.
Doing this will allow these experiments to be expanded to the \lrg data sizes.
Finally, we follow \citet{adil23} in excluding languages with the largest alphabet size (64 characters) from our experiments, but future work should include these as well.
